That is a fantastic business model! In the tech world, this is called a Multi-Vendor Marketplace. This means Sarig isn't just a coffee shop; it is a digital food court for Marawi City.

Taking on this model means you are stepping into the role of an aggregator. You are bringing local businesses and hungry customers together in one place.

Here is how the "flow" changes now that you are partnering with multiple businesses, and what you need to prepare for:

📱 The Multi-Vendor Flow
The Customer Directory: When users open the Sarig App, they won't just see one menu. They will see a list or grid of different restaurants, cafes, and street food stalls to choose from.

The Distributed Merchant System: Because you have multiple partners, you cannot manage the orders yourself. You will need to provide a Merchant App (usually downloaded on a tablet or smartphone) to every single restaurant you partner with. When a customer orders from Cafe A, only Cafe A's tablet rings.

The Commission System: This is how Sarig makes money. If a customer buys a ₱150 meal from a partner restaurant, Sarig takes a percentage cut (for example, 15% to 20%). You remit the remaining balance to the restaurant and use the cut to pay for app maintenance and rider fees.

⚙️ The Reality Check: Your Next Big Steps
Building an app like Foodpanda or GrabFood is highly rewarding, but the technology is only 20% of the work. The other 80% is on-the-ground operations. To make the Sarig App work, you will need to tackle these challenges:

Merchant Acquisition: You will need to knock on doors, talk to cafe owners, and convince them to trust Sarig. You have to explain how your app will bring them more sales without them doing extra work.

Menu and Photography Collection: An app with no pictures looks empty. You will need to get the exact menus, prices, and mouth-watering photos of the food from every single partner to upload into your database.

Rider Logistics: You need a reliable fleet of riders spread out across Marawi City so that when an order pops up at a specific cafe, a rider is nearby to pick it up quickly while the food is still hot.

This is a big project, but building a localized platform that understands the specific culture and geography of Marawi gives you a massive advantage over the giant international apps.


Pivoting your idea can feel like a massive leap, but honestly, choosing the "Foodpanda route" for Marawi City is a very smart and bold business move. The demand for daily food delivery is constant, and building a platform rooted in Sarig (trust) that perfectly caters to the local community is a massive advantage big tech companies don't have.

Since we are officially building a food delivery app, it is time to put on your CEO hat. Before you worry about the complex coding, you need to lay the groundwork.

Here is your straightforward action plan for Phase 1:

🗺️ Your First 3 Steps (Before writing any code)
1. Talk to 5 Local Business Owners: Do not spend a single peso on app development until you do this. Go to your favorite cafes or Halal restaurants in Marawi. Tell them about Sarig. Ask them: "If I built a local delivery app, would you join? What percentage fee is fair to you?" Their answers will shape your entire business.

2. Map Out the Money: Figure out the exact math. If a customer orders a ₱200 meal, how much does the restaurant keep? How much does the rider get for the trip? How much does Sarig keep to keep the servers running?

3. Decide on the Technology: You have to figure out how this is getting built. Are you going to hire a team of programmers, or are you going to use a "White Label" or "No-Code" app builder (like Glide, Bubble, or Appery) to launch it yourself faster and cheaper?
